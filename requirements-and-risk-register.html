Closed-Loop Smart Diabetic-Foot Prevention System

Requirements, safety and risk register (draft v0.2 for review)

Status of this document: a working draft for review by a supervisor, clinician or engineer. Items marked TBD need a decision from a reviewer. Numeric targets are proposed prototype targets, not clinically validated values.

1. Purpose and scope

A laboratory prototype of a sensorized insole that detects sustained plantar-pressure hotspots, adjusts pneumatic support to redistribute pressure, measures again, and keeps or reverses the change.

In scope (3-month prototype): bench testing of sensing, pneumatic actuation, closed-loop control, dashboard and data logging, aimed at ulcer prevention.

Out of scope for this phase: treatment of existing wounds, testing on active wounds, electrical stimulation, drug delivery, any diagnostic or clinical-effectiveness claim, and any use on people before ethics approval.

Claim to be used: "Existing diabetic-foot technologies generally monitor plantar pressure or temperature and provide warnings. This project investigates automatic, feedback-controlled pressure redistribution using a multimodal sensorized insole."

Changes since v0.1: the status table now covers the safety monitors, CSV replay, firmware skeleton and bench test plan added to the project; the safety table has a status column; the artifacts table was updated. No requirement or target value was changed.

5. Milestone acceptance criteria

Milestone Pass when
Month 1 All sensor locations can be measured and displayed live
Month 2 The system detects a simulated hotspot, adjusts support automatically, and verifies the result
Month 3 Bench validation complete: accuracy, repeatability, drift, response time, leakage, false activation, 1,000+ cycles, and a documented safety review

7. Assumptions and open questions for the reviewer

Assumptions used so far (all adjustable and unvalidated):

  • A hotspot threshold of 400 kPa, persistence of 2 s, and deflation steps of 35% then 20% are demo values.
  • Neighbouring zones receive a share of the pressure removed from a deflated zone; the coefficient is invented for the simulation.
  • Fill and vent valves are normally closed, so bladders hold pressure when power is lost.
  • The walking-load model (a half-sine per sensor, heel first, about 1.1 s per stride, 1.5 s peak-hold window) is a schematic stand-in for real gait data.
  • Temperature sensing uses 10 kΩ thermistors; the pump and valves are assumed to be 5 V parts.

Questions for a clinician or diabetic-foot specialist:

  1. Which foot regions are highest priority for a first prototype?
  2. What pressure and duration patterns would you consider worth acting on?
  3. What temperature difference between sites is meaningful?
  4. What insole thickness and stiffness would patients tolerate?
  5. What would make you trust, or distrust, a device that adjusts support automatically?
  6. What pressure should a bladder never exceed against the foot?
  7. What would be required before any test on people?

Questions for an electronics or mechanical reviewer:

  1. Are the board's power, actuator and sensing sections sound? Please check IC pin numbers against datasheets.
  2. Is the 2.5 A actuator rail appropriate for the chosen pump and valves?
  3. Is the internal ADC accurate enough, or is an external ADC needed?
  4. Which bladder construction is most likely to meet the leak and cycling targets?
